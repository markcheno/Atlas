The FlightFinder Occurrence Corpus: harmonising 128 aviation-safety source codes into one deduplicated cross-national accident dataset

Denys Kolomiiets 

Affiliation: FlightFinder / himaxym.com, Lisbon, Portugal 

denys@himaxym.com

September 6, 2026

Abstract

National aviation-investigation databases are deep but territorial; global compilations
such as the Aviation Safety Network and the Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives are
hand-curated, apply their own inclusion thresholds, and publish no per-record lineage.
The FlightFinder Occurrence Corpus harmonizes 279,485 occurrences and 131,882
attached narratives from 128 source codes — national accident-investigation
authorities, occurrence-reporting systems and four global compilations. Records are
mapped into one schema; each normalized record reduces to exactly one key from an
ordered ladder — event signature, registration and date, rounded position and date,
or source and native identifier — so a missing field never creates a link, and the
key is the occurrence identifier. Records the ladder cannot reach are scored in a
blocked pass into an explicit merge table and applied only within an automatically
approved score band or after a person confirms them. Field conflicts follow a
documented source ranking; one provenance row per contributing source — its native
identifier and URL — is retained, with attribution and licence terms per source code.
Linkage error is measured on five adjudicated samples totalling 200 pairs. A
recorded uniform random draw of 60 from the 3,441 merges the automatic
band applies as it ships found no error; the exact two-sided 95 %
Clopper–Pearson upper bound on its false-merge rate is 6.0 %, or 205
pairs. The measured error covers the scored band only; the ladder’s deterministic
joins are not adjudicated. The corpus has no exposure denominators and inherits the
under-reporting of its sources. It is offered as a base for cross-national safety
statistics, source-disagreement studies and narrative text mining; a frozen,
DOI-referenced snapshot accompanies the live API.

Keywords: aviation safety; accident database; record linkage; deduplication; data integration; open data; ICAO ADREP

1 Introduction

Aviation is among the most intensively investigated activities in modern transport, yet the records of that investigation are fragmented in a way that limits what can be learned from them. Each contracting state to the Chicago Convention maintains, under ICAO Annex 13, an authority that investigates accidents and serious incidents in its territory and publishes the results; many states additionally run mandatory or voluntary occurrence-reporting systems for events below the accident threshold. The resulting databases are large, authoritative and, taken one at a time, well documented [1, 2]. They are, however, defined by where an event happened and which authority chose to investigate it, not by the population of events a researcher might wish to study. A fatal accident involving an aircraft registered in one state, operated by a carrier from a second and crashing in a third may appear in three authorities’ files under three identifiers, three occurrence classes and three counts of fatalities, while a comparable accident in a state whose investigation authority publishes little may reach no published final report at all: of the 269 commercial accidents the IATA Accident Classification Task Force classified for 2018–2023, International Air Transport Association and International
Coordinating Council of Aerospace Industries Associations and International
Federation of Air Line Pilots’ Associations [3] report that 57 % resulted in one. That figure bounds the availability of final reports among events already known to the classifiers; it is not a statement about which events reach a database, since an event with no final report can still enter one through a preliminary report, an occurrence-reporting system or a compilation.

Researchers who need a global view have therefore turned to volunteer- or commercially-curated compilations, principally the Aviation Safety Network (ASN) list of airliner and corporate-jet occurrences [4] and the Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives (B3A) [5]. These underpin the best-known long-run risk series [6] and much of the machine-learning literature on accident severity. They have three limitations that motivate the present work. First, they are curated to inclusion thresholds of their own rather than to a reporting mandate — B3A admits aircraft of six seats including crew and above, and the ASN curated database is an airliner and corporate-jet list — so what they contain is an editorial decision that the user cannot re-derive. (The ASN Wikibase, which is what this corpus ingests, is far broader and reaches light aircraft, rotorcraft, gliders and unmanned aircraft; the two ASN products must not be conflated.) Second, they are single-curated and closed-provenance: a record cites, at best, a report, and a user cannot tell which authorities described the event or how disagreements between them were resolved. Third, because part of their input is press coverage, their completeness is correlated with media salience, which has risen over time even as accident frequency has fallen [7]. Oster et al. [8] catalogue the further analytical consequences: shifting definitions, incomparable denominators and non-uniform reporting across countries, none of which a single-source database can quantify from within.

The alternative is to build the global view directly from the official sources and to treat the fragmentation itself as data. That is the approach taken by the FlightFinder Occurrence Corpus described here. The corpus ingests occurrence records from 128 registered source codes—national accident-investigation boards, civil-aviation-authority occurrence-reporting systems, regional bodies where they publish record-level data, and four global compilations that are ingested as sources rather than held aside (Section 3.1)—maps them into a single schema, links records that describe the same aircraft’s involvement in the same physical event, and publishes for every resulting occurrence the complete set of contributing sources with their licence terms. Linking is deterministic wherever a positive identifier exists — an ordered ladder of keys rather than a scored comparison of pairs — and falls back to a reduced Fellegi–Sunter scheme [9] for the cohort that carries no identifier at all: the same blocked comparison and the same separation of an automatically-applied band from a clerical-review queue, but with weights set by hand rather than estimated, which is a heuristic scorer in their shape and not their method (Section 4.6). The corpus dictates that reduction. The fields a full model would weigh are absent on most records, with operator populated on 41.5 % and manufacturer on 0.4 %, so estimating their agreement probabilities would calibrate a comparison that rarely happens. Linkage error is measured on adjudicated samples rather than asserted, and Section 4.7 reports what each sample could and could not see. The corpus is not a new investigation of any event; it is a documented aggregation of existing investigations whose reported statistics can be recomputed from a frozen snapshot, and it inherits their coverage and their errors. What it adds is scale, a common vocabulary and provenance. What it does not achieve is balance: four NTSB exports supply 60.8 % of its source records, so it is a United States dataset with substantial international coverage rather than a global one, and Section 7.6 states what follows from that.

Because this is a data paper, we organize it around design goals rather than research questions. The goals were fixed before the validation sample was drawn and are the standard against which Section 6 reports.

DG1 

Breadth with provenance. Include every source that publishes record-level occurrence data under terms permitting redistribution of at least structured fields — official authorities in the first instance, and the global compilations that cover what no authority publishes — and retain on every canonical occurrence the identity and URL of one record per contributing source, with the attribution text and licence resolved through its source code.

DG2 

Deduplication with measured error. Resolve records describing the same event into one occurrence by a documented rule rather than a fitted model — the deterministic ladder is stated here in full, the scoring pass that bands the residue is stated as rules but not as numbers — publish the adjudicated decisions rather than a model fit, and report the error rate of the automatic band together with the sampling frame it was measured on. Recall is explicitly out of scope: the design errs toward under-merging, and detecting the duplicates it leaves apart would require an external list the corpus does not have.

DG3 

A minimal harmonized schema. Map each source’s native occurrence class, severity, aircraft type and location fields into a small target vocabulary aligned with the ICAO ADREP taxonomy, with the mapping rule documented as implemented — a closed allow-list of accepted values and a stated default for everything else, not a per-source crosswalk — and an explicit rule for choosing canonical values when sources disagree.

DG4 

Reproducibility and versioning. Expose the corpus through a documented API and a frozen, DOI-referenced snapshot such that every figure in this paper can be regenerated from stated calls, and such that later versions are distinguishable from the one described here.

The contributions of the paper are as follows.

(i) 

A source registry of 128 source codes — national investigation authorities, occurrence-reporting systems and four global compilations — each with a coverage window, a state and an attribution string (Section 3).

(ii) 

A construction pipeline documented stage by stage—ingestion, normalization, deterministic key-ladder linkage, source-rank field resolution and occurrence-class mapping—with the linkage model stated formally, and with the limits of that specification stated: the ladder can be re-implemented from this paper, the scoring pass that assigns the merge bands cannot (Section 4).

(iii) 

Linkage error measured on 200 adjudicated pairs across five samples, with the sampling frame of each stated and the selection of sample 4 recorded as a seeded random draw, and a per-source overlap matrix (Sections 5 and 6).

(iv) 

A reproducibility appendix giving the exact API calls, deduplication rules and derived-variable definitions needed to re-derive every table and figure from the deposited snapshot, and stating which of them the deposit does not support (Appendix A).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews existing occurrence databases, the record-linkage literature and data-descriptor conventions. Section 3 describes the source material and the published schema. Section 4 specifies the construction pipeline and Section 5 the validation design. Section 6 reports corpus statistics and validation outcomes against the design goals. Section 7 discusses interpretation, known biases and intended uses, and Section 8 concludes.

2 Related work

2.1 Existing aviation-occurrence databases

Quantitative aviation-safety research has long depended on a small number of
institutional databases, each defined by a jurisdiction, a reporting mandate
and an inclusion threshold rather than by the population of events it aims to
describe. The NTSB aviation database is the canonical national source: it
covers civil accidents and selected incidents since 1962 within the United
States, its territories and international waters, together with foreign
investigations in which the NTSB served as accredited representative, and its
own documentation notes that minor pre-2001 cases were purged and that
location and airport fields are absent before 1982 [1].
Oster et al. [8] review the analytical problems this creates:
denominators (departures, flight hours) are collected by other agencies under
different definitions, accident classifications shift over time, and
cross-country comparison is hampered by non-uniform reporting. In Europe,
Regulation (EU) 376/2014 routes mandatory and voluntary occurrence reports
from 65 authorities into the European Central Repository through the ECCAIRS
platform, which encodes events with the ICAO ADREP taxonomy and now holds
roughly 2.3 million occurrences [2]; the repository is,
however, not open to the public at record level.

Two non-governmental compilations fill the resulting gap for global work. The
Aviation Safety Network, maintained by the Flight Safety Foundation, records
airliner, corporate-jet and military-transport accidents and hijackings since
1919 in its curated database, and a far larger Wikibase — more than
258,000 entries — that extends to light aircraft, helicopters, gliders,
balloons and unmanned aircraft [4]. It is the Wikibase,
not the curated database, that the present corpus ingests. The Bureau of
Aircraft Accidents Archives (B3A) holds more than 38,000 civil and military
files from 1918 onward, with an inclusion floor of six seats including crew,
[5]. Both are curated by hand from official reports and
press coverage, neither publishes a source lineage per record, and their
inclusion thresholds (passenger capacity or hull loss) exclude the large
majority of general-aviation events that dominate national authorities’
files. Barnett [6] illustrates the analytical practice built
on these sources: worldwide death-risk-per-boarding trends for 1968–2017 are
computed from the ASN airliner list combined with traffic denominators, with
the author explicitly flagging jurisdictions where reporting may be
incomplete. More recently, Omrani et al. [10] compared machine
learning severity models trained separately on NTSB, TSB Canada and ATSB
records for 2008–2023 and found that field availability and coding
conventions differ enough across the three authorities to change model
behaviour—an early quantitative signal that harmonisation is a research
problem in its own right. Crowd-sourced ADS-B archives such as the OpenSky
Network [11] have produced open, versioned
datasets of flight movements and derived safety proxies (emergency squawks,
go-arounds), demonstrating the data-descriptor model for aviation but
covering trajectories, not investigated occurrences. Narrative text has been
mined at scale within single reporting systems
[12], again without cross-authority linkage.

2.2 Record linkage and deduplication

Merging occurrence files from many authorities is an instance of record
linkage, whose statistical foundations were laid by
Fellegi and Sunter [9]: pairs of records are scored by the likelihood
ratio of agreement patterns under the match and non-match hypotheses, and two
thresholds partition pairs into links, non-links and a clerical-review zone.
Christen [13] gives the standard engineering treatment—
pre-processing, blocking, comparison functions for names, dates and
locations, classification and evaluation—while Papadakis et al. [14]
survey blocking and filtering methods that keep pairwise comparison tractable
when the candidate space is quadratic in corpus size. Modern
implementations estimate the Fellegi–Sunter parameters by
expectation–maximisation without labelled training pairs;
Enamorado et al. [15] show that this scales to tens of millions
of administrative records while yielding calibrated match probabilities and
uncertainty for downstream analyses. Bayesian formulations treat the linkage
structure as a latent bipartite matching or partition, propagating linkage
uncertainty and enforcing one-to-one constraints
[16]. Binette and Steorts [17] review the field and
emphasise two points directly relevant here: deduplication within a single
file and linkage across files are the same problem when records are clustered
to latent entities, and evaluation requires a held-out labelled sample rather
than agreement rates alone. Aviation occurrences are an unusually favourable
linkage domain—date, registration mark, ICAO aircraft type and location are
near-unique jointly—but registration re-use, transliterated place names,
time-zone-shifted dates and authorities reporting the same event under
different classifications (accident, serious incident, incident) generate
precisely the ambiguous pairs for which probabilistic scoring and manual
adjudication of the review zone were designed.

2.3 Data-paper conventions and documentation

The data-descriptor genre has matured into a distinct publication type with
its own norms. Wilkinson et al. [18] set out the FAIR principles
(findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) that data journals now
require: persistent identifiers, rich machine-readable metadata, standard
vocabularies and explicit licences. Gebru et al. [19] propose the
“datasheet” checklist—motivation, composition, collection process,
preprocessing, uses, distribution and maintenance—which has become the de
facto structure for documenting provenance and known biases of a compiled
dataset. Within aviation, Noort et al. [20] show what a Data in Brief
descriptor for accident-derived material looks like: a small, fully
annotated corpus with a codebook, validation statistics and a reuse
statement. The OpenSky descriptor in Earth System Science Data
[11] is the closest aviation precedent for a
large, continuously updated corpus published with versioned DOIs and an API.
None of these treat multi-source integration and per-record provenance as
the central contribution.

2.4 Under-reporting and coverage bias

Any compilation of official records inherits the coverage of the reporting
systems it draws on, and that coverage is known to be incomplete and uneven.
At the level of the investigation itself, an ICAO Assembly working paper
reports that only 57 % of the 269 commercial accidents classified by the
IATA Accident Classification Task Force for 2018–2023 had a published final
report [3]; missingness is concentrated in particular
states, so global datasets built from published reports are systematically
thinner in exactly the regions with the highest accident rates. Below the
accident threshold, reporting is voluntary or weakly enforced:
Gao et al. [21] model the joint time series of voluntary reports and
accidents in US commercial aviation and find reporting volume responds to
accident occurrence rather than the reverse, while
Kioulepoglou et al. [22] document, through interviews with
Greek pilots, the fear of sanction, perceived futility and workload that
suppress incident reports—mechanisms that vary by operator and country and
therefore induce spatially structured under-coverage. Coverage is further
distorted upstream of official channels: van der Meer et al. [7] show that
news attention per fatal aviation accident rose between 1996 and 2017 even
as frequency fell, so sources that depend on press coverage (as the
volunteer-curated compilations partly do) over-represent salient events.
Oster et al. [8] note the same problem for cross-national
comparisons. Multi-source datasets offer a partial remedy: when the same
event is captured independently by several authorities, the pattern of
overlap can in principle be used to estimate what any single source misses.
Section 5.2 explains why that estimate is not
offered for this corpus: the compilations that would serve as the second list
are ingested as sources, so the lists are not independent.

2.5 Research gap

No existing resource combines (i) record-level data from more than a handful
of official investigation authorities, (ii) an explicit, reproducible
deduplication step that is documented rather than fitted, with a measured
error rate and its adjudications published,
and (iii) per-record provenance that retains every contributing source. The
ASN and B3A compilations are global but single-curated and
closed-provenance; NTSB, ECCAIRS and their peers are deep but
jurisdictional; OpenSky-derived datasets describe traffic rather than
investigated occurrences; and we are aware of no published
application of the record-linkage literature to cross-authority aviation
occurrence data. The FlightFinder Occurrence Corpus
addresses this by harmonising 128 source codes into one schema, resolving
duplicates with a deterministic key ladder backed by an explicit merge table
whose adjudicated decisions are published, and attaching source attribution to
each of 279,485 events and 131,882 attached narratives. The corpus borrows the Fellegi–Sunter decision
structure for the records its key ladder cannot reach — blocked comparison,
per-field agreement, and the separation of confident links from a clerical-review queue — while
deliberately omitting the parameter estimation that the implementations above
provide, because the fields such a model would weigh are missing on most
records and the comparison that matters here is cross-lingual normalisation
rather than string similarity. Beyond serving as an
analysis-ready global dataset, the retained source overlap enables direct
measurement of cross-authority disagreement on the 10,757 events that more
than one authority recorded — but not capture–recapture bounds on events
every authority missed, since the two global compilations that would serve as
independent lists are themselves ingested.

3 Data

3.1 Source material and provenance

The corpus is built from the occurrence databases, accident lists and report archives published by 128 registered source codes. Most are official in the strict sense — an accident-investigation authority established under Annex 13, or a civil aviation authority operating an occurrence-reporting system — but four are not: the Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives, the Aviation Safety Network, Wikidata and SKYbrary are volunteer or community compilations, and they are ingested as sources rather than held aside. That is a change from an earlier design of this corpus and it has a consequence the paper returns to in Section 5.2: because those compilations are inside the corpus, they cannot serve as the independent lists an external coverage check would require. Together they supply 23.8 % of source records. Each source is registered with a stable code (the source field of the API), its homepage, an attribution string and a licence field. A state is recorded for the 123 codes that are national authorities; the four global compilations and the interstate MAK carry none, because the field would be false rather than empty. One licence entry is not a grant: see Section 7.6.1. Table 1 summarizes it by source code; the five largest are three of the four NTSB exports [1], the Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives and the Aviation Safety Network, which between them supply 83.4 % of source records; the largest national authority after the NTSB is the United Kingdom AAIB at 2.3 %. 39.2 % of source records come from outside the NTSB exports and 9.4 % from outside the ten largest codes altogether.

Two features of this distribution matter more than any regional cut would.
First, the corpus is dominated by one state: four NTSB source codes supply
60.8 % of all source records, so any unqualified statement about
“global” aviation safety drawn from it is a statement about the United States
with a tail. Second, the count of 128 is of source codes, not of
authorities: NTSB alone occupies four of them because its bulk data is
published in several incompatible historical formats, and four of the remaining
codes are the global compilations rather than investigation bodies at all.

Source records were retrieved by 128 source-specific adapters, the most recent extraction being 2026-08 from bulk downloads where offered (for example the NTSB avall database export) and otherwise from paginated public query interfaces or published report indexes (the retrieval route is a property of each adapter and is not recorded in the registry; supplementary Table S1 gives the code, attribution, state, homepage, record count and event-year window per source, which is what the registry does hold). Each retrieved record is written to a staging table keyed on the source and the source’s own identifier, with a timestamp of the last write, before any transformation, so that each canonical field can be traced to the source record it was derived from as that record stood at the most recent re-sync (Section 4.2).

3.2 Coverage window, unit of analysis and inclusion

The corpus spans event dates from 1902 to 2026; the effective start of dense coverage differs by source and is reported per source in Table S1 and by region in Table 1. Two units of analysis coexist. A source record is one row as published by one authority, identified by (source, id). An occurrence is the canonical event produced by the linkage step, identified by the API id, and is the unit for all counts reported in this paper unless stated otherwise. An occurrence is therefore one aircraft’s involvement in an event, not the event itself. The second rung of the key ladder keys 216,528 of the 279,485 occurrences (77.5 %) on registration and date (Section 4.4), so two airframes in one mid-air collision receive two different keys by construction and remain two occurrences; the collision guard of Section 4.6 exists to keep the fallback scorer from undoing that. Users counting events rather than involvements must collapse such occurrences themselves — the midair_collision, Collision With Aircraft - Both In Flight and Airborne collision categories (2,874 occurrences, of which 1,528 fall in 726 same-day, same-country groups of two or more) are where to start. The same holds for fatality sums: where each authority reports the combined toll of a collision on each airframe’s record, that toll is counted once per occurrence. Its magnitude is not determinable from the deposit — of the 332 same-day pairs carrying an identical non-zero fatalities_total, 248 have occupants null on both sides and no narrative text is deposited, so a shared toll cannot be told from two equal tolls — and it is reported here as a property of the unit, not corrected. An occurrence is included if at least one contributing source record satisfies that source’s own publication criteria and has a parseable event date; records with no usable event date are dropped during the build rather than quarantined; there is no staging table, and the count appears only in the build log. Records that describe the same event but are classified differently by different authorities (for instance an accident by the state of occurrence and a serious incident by the state of registry) are linked, not excluded, but the classification conflict is not retained: the priority rule of Section 4.8 writes one harmonized class per occurrence and the losing record’s class is not persisted, so the disagreement is visible neither in the API nor in the deposit (Section 6.3). Purely administrative entries, duplicate republications within a single source, and events involving no aircraft (ground-handling injuries with no aircraft involvement, where a source records them) are excluded by per-source rules documented in the adapter code (the adapters themselves are in the repository named under Data and code availability).

The corpus contains no exposure denominators. Flight hours, departures and fleet sizes are collected by other agencies under definitions that are not aligned with occurrence reporting [8], and attaching them is the subject of a companion paper on exposure-adjusted rates by aircraft family, in preparation. Counts from this corpus are therefore counts of reported and published events; they are not rates and should not be interpreted as such without an external denominator.

3.3 Published schema

Table 2 lists the fields of the two published objects. An Occurrence carries the canonical values selected by the priority rule of Section 4.8 and the array sources[] of contributing source records. A Narrative is a per-source object, never merged across authorities, so that text is always attributable to exactly one publisher and one licence; it carries the source’s own phase_of_flight string unharmonized, and a facts{} object that repeats the canonical occurrence the narrative resolves to, for callers who fetch a narrative without fetching its event. facts{} is not a per-source field store: no source-specific keys are carried anywhere in the published objects, so structured detail beyond the harmonized schema — injury tables and weather, for instance — has to be read from the source through the URL in event_sources. Where a source publishes a longer article or report page, an article sub-object carries its title, a short excerpt, the canonical URL and, subject to the source’s policy and the API tier, the full text.

3.4 Known biases

Three sources of bias are inherent in the material and are quantified where possible in Section 6. Coverage bias arises because the corpus can only contain events that an authority investigated and published; International Air Transport Association and International
Coordinating Council of Aerospace Industries Associations and International
Federation of Air Line Pilots’ Associations [3] report that 43 % of the commercial accidents the IATA Accident Classification Task Force had already classified for 2018–2023 reached no published final report, and the missingness is concentrated by state. The denominator there is events known to the classifiers, not events in the world, and a missing final report does not by itself keep an event out of this corpus. Threshold bias arises because each source applies its own inclusion rule—the NTSB purged minor pre-2001 cases [1], occurrence-reporting systems include events far below the accident threshold [2]—so that the mix of occurrence classes varies by source and by period independently of the underlying event population. Reporting-culture bias arises below the accident threshold, where voluntary reporting volume responds to salient accidents [21] and is suppressed by fear of sanction and perceived futility in ways that vary by operator and state [22]. The corpus does not correct any of these; it makes them visible by stratifying every count by source and occurrence class.

4 Corpus construction pipeline

The pipeline has six stages, executed in order and re-run in full at each release: source registry, ingestion, normalization, deterministic key-ladder linkage, source-rank field resolution, and occurrence-type and severity mapping. Each stage writes a table keyed on (source, id) or on the canonical id, so that any published value can be traced back through the stages to the source record as it stood at the last re-sync.

4.1 Source registry

The registry is the controlled list of authorities from which records may enter the corpus. Adding a source requires recording its code, legal name, state or organization, homepage, the licence or terms-of-use text applicable to structured data and to narratives, the derived policy, the retrieval method, the native occurrence-class vocabulary it publishes (which is recorded for reference only: it is not mapped term by term, see Section 4.9), and its state and homepage. The registry also holds a priority tier used in Section 4.8. The registry currently holds 130 codes. 123 follow the uniform adapter contract; the remaining seven codes — mak, atsb, baaa, ntsb, wikidata, aviation-safety and skybrary — keep their own tables because their published formats do not fit it. Those seven are codes, not organizations: the NTSB’s other three exports (ntsb1962, ntsbpre, ntsbaar) follow the uniform contract and are counted among the 123, so one authority spans both groups. 128 of the 130 have actually contributed records to this snapshot; bdca and dinacpy are registered but have delivered nothing yet, so every count in this paper is over the contributing 128 and not over the registry (Table 1, Table S1). Registration is not the same relation as contribution and the two must not be quoted interchangeably. The contributing codes carry 117 distinct state values, of which one is the placeholder ZZ used by pubrec, a heterogeneous public-records channel that belongs to no single state; 116 are real states. A code is also not an authority: the NTSB alone occupies four codes, one per published export. The registry records a code, an attribution string, a state and a homepage per source; it does not classify them by Annex 13 role, and this paper does not report such a breakdown because it would have to be invented rather than read. Redistribution terms are likewise held as free text rather than as a licence class — 128 distinct attribution strings and 127 distinct strings in the license field across the contributing codes, most of them organisation names rather than licences (Section 7.6.1) — so narrative reuse is decided per source at ingest and not summarised here.

4.2 Ingestion

Each source has an adapter that retrieves records and writes them to a staging table keyed on (source, case_id), where case_id is the source’s own identifier, together with a built_at timestamp of the last write. Adapters are idempotent: a re-run retrieves the current state and updates the existing row in place, keeping the record’s internal identifier stable across re-syncs. The staging table therefore holds each source record as it stood at the most recent re-sync and not a history of its revisions: a source-side amendment — a revised fatality count after a final report, for example — replaces the earlier value, and the earlier value is not retained. A user who needs the pre-revision state must obtain it from the source; the corpus records only the URL. The payload is the source’s own representation—a database row, a CSV line, or an HTML report page reduced to its structured fields and narrative text. Adapters do not interpret content; interpretation begins at normalization. Ingestion is scheduled per source at intervals from daily to quarterly according to the source’s own update cadence, which is configured per adapter rather than stored in the registry and is therefore not tabulated.

4.3 Normalization

Normalization maps each raw payload to a common intermediate record with typed fields and, for each field, a source-specific parser. Four fields are critical to linkage and are treated with care. Event date is parsed to a calendar date in the local time zone of the stated location. The parser records internally whether it resolved a day, a month or only a year. That flag is not carried into the published schema as a field of its own (Table 2), but the distinction is not lost: a date resolved only to a month or a year is stored as a shorter string, so the split is recoverable from event_date itself and is reported in Section 5.4. Sources publishing UTC timestamps are converted using the location’s time zone, because most authorities record local dates and a UTC timestamp near midnight would otherwise disagree by one day with a local-date record of the same event. Registration mark is normalized by upper-casing, removing hyphens, spaces and punctuation, expanding known national-prefix variants (for example the presence or absence of a hyphen after a one- or two-letter prefix), and stripping trailing annotations such as “(2)” used by some authorities to denote re-used marks; the raw string is retained alongside the normalized form. Aircraft type is mapped from free-text manufacturer and model strings to an ICAO Doc 8643 designator by a lookup table built iteratively from the most frequent unresolved strings, with a residual of 84.4 % of occurrences left unresolved (type_icao null) and a family slug assigned from a designator-to-family table. Location is resolved to a country ISO code from the source’s country field where present, otherwise from a gazetteer match on place name and, where available, coordinates; transliteration variants are handled by matching on the ASCII-folded form. Operator names are ASCII-folded, lower-cased and stripped of legal-form suffixes for comparison but retained as published for display.

4.4 Deterministic linkage by key ladder

Records from different sources that describe the same event are identified
deterministically, not probabilistically. Each normalized record is reduced to
exactly one key, and records sharing a key are one occurrence. The key is the
occurrence identifier: there is no surrogate id assigned separately from the
linkage decision, so an identifier changes if and only if the fields it is
built from change.

The ladder is evaluated in order, and the first rung that applies wins.

1. 

Event signature, for the three authorities that publish
preliminary, interim and final reports of one event under different case
identifiers (AAIA Hong Kong, AAIIB Philippines, AICPNG Papua New Guinea):
evt:<source>:<date>:<signature>, where the signature is the first 40 characters of the
narrative with report-tier boilerplate removed. These sources are keyed here
first regardless of what other fields the row carries, because their
registration arrives from a fallible PDF-extraction step that can succeed for
one tier of an event and fail for another — checking registration first let a
partial extraction give one tier a different key from its siblings, recreating
the duplicate the rung exists to prevent.

2. 

Registration and date:
reg:<normalized registration>:<date>, when the record carries a registration that survives a
placeholder filter. The filter is an exact word list rather than a heuristic:
dashless all-letter strings are legitimate Brazilian tails written without the
separator, so a “looks like a word” rule would silently delete real airframes’
histories.

3. 

Rounded position and date:
geo:<lat>:<lon>:<date> at one decimal place, when coordinates are present and are
not within 0.05^{\circ} of the origin. Sources ship 10^{-6} for “position
unknown”, and testing for exact zero rather than at the key’s own precision
once collapsed every coordinate-less accident sharing a date into a single
occurrence.

4. 

Source and native identifier:
src:<source>:<native id>,
otherwise. A record that reaches this rung cannot merge with anything, by
construction.

An earlier rung on operator and date was removed after audit: it merged
distinct accidents flown by the same operator on the same day.

The design choice this ladder embodies is that a missing field must never
create a link. Every rung above the last requires a positive identifier —
a registration, a position, an event signature — and a record carrying none
of them falls to a key that is unique by construction rather than being matched
on the fields it does have. The corpus therefore errs toward under-merging, and
Section 4.5 describes what is done about the residue.

4.5 Post-merge recovery passes

Three passes run after the ladder, each addressing a class of duplicate the
ladder cannot reach on its own. They are ordered, and the order matters: each
pass can create keys that the next one uses as merge targets.

Timezone and registration backfill.

SKYbrary records local time
where some primary sources record UTC, and carries registrations for events
whose primary source omitted them. A pass reconciles \pm 1 day offsets and
backfills registrations onto src:-keyed occurrences, subject to a
conservative type-compatibility check that prefers a false negative to a wrong
merge.

Registration recovery from free text.

The registration rung is
reached only when an adapter supplied a registration field. Several authorities
bury the tail in prose — the BEA and ANSV in French and Italian narratives,
the TSB inside the type string (“Cessna 208B Caravan, C-GMVB”), and the ANSV
case identifiers degrade to a bare country prefix that the placeholder filter
correctly rejects. A pass recovers registration-shaped tokens from the type and
narrative fields and merges only when the recovered key lands on a same-date
occurrence created independently by another source. The key hit is the
validation: a tail-shaped string proves nothing, while a string that resolves
to another authority’s record for the same day is evidence. When this pass last ran it scanned 66,241 geo:- and
src:-keyed rows and merged 182. That population is a run-time figure and
the snapshot cannot reproduce it: the merges it made have since collapsed rows, so the
deposited corpus carries 62,674 such keys (40,656 geo: and
22,018 src:) rather than the pre-merge count. The 182 merges are
likewise applied and not separately recoverable. Both numbers are reported as measured at
run time and are marked here as not reproducible from the deposit, rather than quoted as
though a reader could check them.

Adjudicated merges.

The largest duplicate cohort produces no shared
key at all: records where at least one side reaches the source rung, having no usable
registration and no usable position. Two global compilations rewrite the same official
reports, so one accident exists twice with nothing the ladder can match on.
These are proposed by the scoring pass of
Section 4.6, recorded in an explicit merge table with
a status, and applied at build time only when the status is
auto or confirmed. A table rather than a further rung, for
three reasons: a wrong rung is discovered only as a missing accident, whereas a
row names both sides, and deleting it restores them on the next build of the
closed database, where both records’ field values still exist (the deposit
carries the absorbed record’s source identity and URL but not its field values,
so a reader of the snapshot can name what was merged but cannot undo it) —
with one further qualification stated below; the table is where a review queue
can live; and it lets this paper report how many events were merged by the
automatic band and how many by a recorded decision.

Chains are resolved before application. One event in the corpus existed as
three occurrences and contributed its death toll three times; a table naming
 a\to b and b\to c , applied in table order, would leave a pointing at a
record already removed. Every absorbed identifier is therefore resolved to its
final survivor first, and a cycle — which would require one record both to
survive and not to survive — is dropped entire rather than applied by halves.

One consequence must be stated for anyone joining the deposited merge table to
the snapshot: survivor_id is the survivor as of the row’s writing and
not a final identifier. 85 of the 4,068 rows (34
auto and 51 pending) name a survivor_id
that does not appear in events.jsonl.gz at all — 84 of them
TSB records and one from ntsbpre. None is a wrong merge. In all
34 auto rows and 37 of the pending ones the
survivor’s native record now sits under the row’s own absorbed_id,
because a later registration-recovery pass re-keyed it and reversed the
direction the row records; the remaining 14 sit under a third
registration key. A further 8 pending rows name an
absorbed_id that is itself absent, each carrying
fan-demoted. The consumer rule is therefore to resolve either side
through event_sources.csv.gz on (source, native_id)
rather than by matching identifiers directly.

4.6 Proposing merges for the unkeyable cohort

Pairs for the merge table are proposed by a scoring pass, run offline rather
than inside the corpus build: the table holds human decisions, and
regenerating it on every build would either overwrite them or force the build
to reason about them.

Blocking is exact on event date and death toll, with at least two deaths. This
is a crude block and it is the only natural one available for this cohort: the
date is the single field every source fills, and the toll is the only other
value two independent rewrites of one official report reliably agree on. A
record with no registration and no position offers nothing else to block on.

The two-death threshold has a consequence that should be stated rather than
left to be inferred, because it names where the residual duplication lives.
A pair below the threshold is never scored, never proposed and never merged:
it is not that the scorer judges it and declines, but that it is never
considered. That is not a small corner of the cohort. Of the 62,674
occurrences the ladder could reach only by its weakest rungs, 46,594
— 74.3 % — carry a known fatality count below two. Whatever
duplication survives in this corpus is therefore concentrated, by
construction, in low-toll occurrences that no source keyed by registration or
position; the adjudicated samples say nothing about that stratum, and the
error rates of Section 6.2 should not be read as
applying to it.

Everything after the block is scoring, and the scheme is a deliberately
reduced Fellegi–Sunter [9]: a blocked comparison, a
per-field agreement vector, a weighted score, and a cut of that score into a
band applied automatically and a band held for clerical review, after the
partition their formulation introduced. What is removed is the parameter
estimation. The m and u probabilities are not
estimated by expectation–maximisation [15]; the
weights are set by hand and the score is a blunt confidence used to order a
queue, not a calibrated posterior. Two further departures should be read as
disqualifying the label rather than qualifying it. The score takes only eleven
discrete values, because it is a sum of fixed rule bonuses and not a
likelihood ratio, so it has no distribution to threshold and no calibration
curve to plot. And the third Fellegi–Sunter tier is not materialised: pairs
scoring below the review band are never written to the merge table, which
therefore contains links and review-queue pairs only. What is implemented is a
blocked heuristic scorer that inherits the shape of their decision rule; the
inferential content of the method is not present, and none of the guarantees
that follow from it are claimed here.

The removal is a decision about this corpus rather than a shortcut. A full
model earns its keep when many fields discriminate and several blocking keys
are available; here the operator field is populated on 41.5 % of
records and manufacturer on 0.4 %, the date is the only natural
blocking key for the cohort in question, and the comparison that matters most
— an airframe written in Cyrillic by one source and Latin by another — is a
normalisation problem that no off-the-shelf implementation solves. Estimating
 m and u over fields that are usually missing would produce well-calibrated
weights for a comparison that rarely happens. The decision structure is worth
keeping; the parameter layer is not, and claiming it would be the failure this
paper exists to avoid. A pair is proposed only when the aircraft type agrees on at least one
significant token and either the operator agrees or the two records
name the same state. The death toll is not a second signal here: it is part of
the block, so every pair reaching the scoring stage already agrees on it. Agreement
is required positively rather than as absence of contradiction, because absence
is cheap in this corpus: the operator field is populated on
41.5 % of records and manufacturer on 0.4 %, so two records can
fail to contradict each other on almost everything.

Type comparison folds Cyrillic OKB designations to Latin and glues a designator
to its number, without which a MAK record and an Aviation Safety Network record
of the same airframe share no token at all.

Guards.

Four rules demote a scoring pair to the review queue rather
than merging it. Each was added because a sample found the failure it prevents,
and each cost merges that would otherwise have been made.

• 

Mass fleet, small toll. Wartime transports of mass operators
were lost in batches, so a same-day, same-toll coincidence is ordinary rather
than remarkable. A sample drawn deliberately from this cell contained four
genuine false merges in thirty — two different C-47s of the same air force,
same day, same toll, one in Indiana and one in Burma, separated only by a
production block that neither compilation records.

• 

Manufacturer agreement without model agreement. A
manufacturer’s name is a token shared by every aircraft it built. Two records
agreeing on Junkers and nothing else were merged, one a Ju 52 and the
other a Ju 88. A conflicting model number under a shared designator prefix now
demotes the pair. It demotes rather than rejects, because a Convair 340
converted to a 580 is one airframe under two designations.

• 

One collision, two airframes. A mid-air collision between two
aircraft of one type and operator produces two records sharing every blocking
field, and both are legitimate because each is one airframe’s
involvement — the unit of analysis defined in
Section 3.2. Nothing in the scoring can separate them, and the
fan guard below does not fire because a clean two-way crossing produces no fan.
A collision marker in either narrative demotes the pair.

• 

Fan. A record appearing in more than one proposal cannot be the
same event as both. At most one is right and the scoring does not say which, so
all of its proposals go to a person.

4.7 Linkage error, measured

Linkage error is measured on adjudicated samples rather than asserted, and
the sampling frame matters more than the headline. Five samples were drawn in
sequence, each in response to what the previous one failed to cover, and the
two guards above came out of that sequence: the false merge and the
undecidable pair found in the third sample became the model-number and
collision guards, both added before any merge reached the corpus. The samples,
their frames, their verdicts and what each can and cannot support are reported
together in Section 6.2 and
Table 4 rather than twice; the design point to carry into the
next section is that the frames are stated because they are not
interchangeable, and that adjudication used the sources’ own published records,
so the resulting figures bound clerical error and not the deeper question of
whether two compilations repeated one upstream mistake.

4.8 Field resolution by source rank

When an occurrence has more than one contributing record, one record wins
outright and its gaps are filled from the others. There is no per-field
election and no Annex 13 role in the rule, for the reason
Section 4.1 gives: the registry does not record what
role a source played in a given occurrence, so a rule that depended on it could
not be evaluated.

What is implemented is a static three-level rank over sources, reflecting
field richness rather than legal standing:

• 

Rank 3 — every national investigation authority. Most reach
this rank through the uniform adapter contract; the NTSB and the ATSB keep
their own tables for format reasons (Section 3.1) but rank
with the rest, which matters because the four NTSB codes alone supply
60.8 % of source records.

• 

Rank 2 — MAK and the Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives.

• 

Rank 1 — Wikidata, the Aviation Safety Network and SKYbrary.

The record from the higher-ranked source becomes the occurrence, and every
field still null on it is filled from the records it beat, in the order they
were seen. is_hull_loss is the one exception and is a disjunction:
if any contributing record reports a hull loss, the occurrence does.

Ties are broken by first arrival, except among the three tier-aware sources of
Section 4.4, where a final report outranks an interim
one and an interim outranks a preliminary. Publication date plays no part.

Two consequences are worth stating because they are not what a reader would
assume. A rank-1 compilation loses to a rank-3 authority on every field they
both populate, even where the compilation is demonstrably more accurate — the
rank is a proxy for richness, not for correctness. And because resolution is
per record rather than per field, an occurrence’s fields are internally
consistent with one source’s account rather than being the best available value
of each; the losing records remain reachable through the provenance list, but
their field values are not retained in the published occurrence.

4.9 Occurrence-type and severity mapping

Each source publishes its own occurrence classification, and the corpus does
not reconcile them. There is no per-source crosswalk table. The mapping is a
closed set of three accepted values — accident, incident,
combat_loss — and anything a source publishes that is not one
of those three becomes accident. A source’s “serious incident”, its
“occurrence”, its national category and its blank field all arrive at the
same value, and nothing records that they did.

The realised distribution follows directly from that default rather than from
the world. The dominance of accident at 99.17 % is a
property of the mapping, not of aviation: it is what a fall-through rule
produces when most sources classify in a vocabulary the corpus does not
recognise. A reader who takes the figure as evidence that 99 % of
recorded occurrences were accidents in the Annex 13 sense will be wrong, and
the error is ours to declare rather than theirs to discover. Three classes occur: accident
(277,157; 99.17 %), incident (2,073;
0.74 %) and combat_loss (255; 0.09 %).

Two consequences follow and both should be read before using the field.
There is no serious_incident class, although Annex 13 defines
one and several sources publish it: the value survives only in the
severity field, on 69 records that are typed
accident. A study filtering on occurrence type will therefore find no
serious incidents at all, and one filtering on severity will find them
mislabelled as accidents. And combat_loss is not a safety
event: it is retained because the underlying sources record shoot-downs and
war losses, and excluded from any rate this paper reports, but a naive
WHERE occurrence_type = ’accident’ both keeps them out and silently
changes the denominator.

severity is derived, not mapped: an occurrence is fatal if the canonical fatalities_total is greater than zero, and is otherwise assigned from the highest injury level and the aircraft damage level reported by the highest-priority record that carries either field (the non-fatal levels are hull_loss (9,743), minor (672), incident (196) and serious_incident (69), each carried through from the source where it publishes one). Occurrences with no injury or damage information from any source receive the severity level unknown, which is a stored value and not a null. Because both fields depend on the mapping rule and on the priority rule, any change to either is versioned with the corpus (Section Data and code availability).

5 Technical validation

Validation addresses design goals DG1 and DG3 directly and DG2 in a form the
original goal did not anticipate, along two axes: the error rate of the linkage
step, measured on adjudicated samples, and the structure of source
overlap. A third axis — coverage against independent compilations — was
planned and is not available;
Section 5.2 explains why, and the explanation is a
result rather than an omission.

5.1 Linkage error on adjudicated samples

Following Christen [13] and Binette and Steorts [17], linkage
quality is measured on adjudicated pairs rather than inferred from model fit.
The measurement here is narrower than those references assume, and the
narrowing is a consequence of the method: a deterministic ladder produces no
match weights to stratify and no thresholds to move, so the stratified
precision-and-recall estimator those papers describe has nothing to attach to.
It does produce clusters — records sharing a key are one occurrence — and
those clusters carry no error estimate here at all. 7,171 reg-keyed
occurrences hold two or more source records joined by the shared
reg: key alone (7,886 source records across all
10,364 multi-record reg: occurrences), and 179
occurrences are joined on the geo rung. All 200 adjudicated pairs are
scorer pairs; not one is a ladder join. The geo rung is the higher risk of the
two, since it joins on date and a position rounded to 0.1 degree, some
11 kilometres at the equator. A later version should draw a uniform
seeded sample from each ladder population and adjudicate it as sample 4 was
adjudicated; no error rate for these joins is asserted here, because none has
been measured.

What is measured is the error rate of the scoring band that merges
automatically, on samples drawn from stated frames. Five samples were
adjudicated, drawn sequentially rather than to a protocol fixed in
advance; Table 4 gives each frame. The rates are not pooled,
because the frames are not nested and a pooled figure would average a stratum
chosen for difficulty against one chosen at random.

Two quantities that would normally accompany such a table are absent and are
named here rather than left to inference. Recall is not measured. The
ladder errs toward leaving records apart, so its characteristic failure is a
duplicate that survives as two occurrences, and nothing in this design detects
one. Inter-adjudicator agreement is not measured. The largest sample was
split into disjoint halves that were adjudicated separately, so no pair was
decided twice and there is no overlap from which to compute a coefficient.

5.2 Why coverage is not assessed against ASN and B3A

An earlier design for this paper assessed external validity against the two
global compilations, the Aviation Safety Network and the Bureau of Aircraft
Accidents Archives, and estimated the events missed by all three lists by
capture–recapture. That design is not reported here, because it cannot be
executed on this corpus, and the reason is worth stating plainly rather than
quietly dropping.

Both compilations are ingested. They are not external lists against
which the corpus can be checked; they are part of it. On the snapshot described
here the Aviation Safety Network is the primary source of 21,242
occurrences (7.6 %) and the Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives of
34,754 (12.4 %); SKYbrary, which the priority rule ranks
lowest, is the primary source of none, and the four compilations together
supply about 23.8 % of source records.
Measuring the corpus against them measures a subset of the corpus against
itself. The overlap term m in a Lincoln–Petersen estimator would not be the
independent recapture the estimator assumes; it would be an artefact of one
ingestion pipeline reading two files.

The failure is not repaired by modelling dependence. A log-linear model with
interaction terms relaxes the independence assumption between genuinely
separate lists; it does not make a list independent of a corpus that contains
it. Nor is the problem confined to the estimator: the same objection voids any
coverage percentage quoted against these two compilations, because the
denominator and the numerator share rows.

What can honestly be said about coverage is narrower and appears in
Section 7.6: the corpus inherits the reporting thresholds and
territorial scope of its contributing authorities, and no statement in this
paper should be read as an estimate of events that no authority recorded.
A genuine external check would require a list assembled independently of every
source the corpus ingests. The United States is the one jurisdiction where such
a check is available in principle, since the NTSB bulk export can be compared
against the corpus’s own NTSB-derived records to estimate ingestion loss rather
than world coverage; that is a narrower claim than the one originally
planned, and it is a check on the pipeline, not on the world.

5.3 Per-source overlap matrix

For DG1 the informative statistic is how much of each source is redundant with others. We compute the 128\times 128 matrix O_{ij} counting occurrences with contributing records from both source i and source j . It is reported as raw counts rather than as a fraction of either source, and it is therefore symmetric: normalizing by the size of source i would give the arguably more useful asymmetric statistic — the share of i that j also covers — but that is not what Figure 3 plots, and the two should not be confused. The matrix is sparse, with its dense cells between the global compilations and between an investigation authority and a compilation that rewrites its reports; the largest off-diagonal cell is 3,354, between the Aviation Safety Network and the Bureau of Aircraft Accidents Archives. A source whose records are all covered by a higher-priority source contributes provenance but not coverage. Neither the per-source count of single-source occurrences nor a list of sources with no overlap at all is computed here; both would follow from event_sources.csv.gz and neither is claimed as a result of this paper.

5.4 Internal consistency

Field-level checks are run on every release. Those the snapshot carries in descriptives.json are the completeness and consistency figures quoted above; the date-precision split is not among them and is stated here instead, computed from events.jsonl.gz: 279,297 occurrences carry a ten-character date string (99.93 %), 48 resolve only to a month and 140 only to a year, and no occurrence is dated after 2026. That is a count of string length, not of validity: the build gate checks that a four-digit year can be read and falls in a plausible range, and never validates the month or the day. Exactly two stored values are not calendar dates — reg:9HVLT:2007-18-20 (month 18, source malta) and a bpea record storing 2018-03-42 (day 42) — so 279,295 of the ten-character values are valid dates. Both are recorded here as errata rather than silently repaired, because the first is baked into that occurrence’s linkage key and correcting it would change its published id. The remaining per-release checks are the fraction of records with resolved type_icao, non-null registration and resolved country; the fraction of fatal occurrences whose class is not accident (which should be near zero under Annex 13 and indicates mapping errors); the number of registrations appearing in more than 5 occurrences (re-used marks or normalization failures); and the count of occurrences dated in the future or before the source’s stated coverage window.

6 Results

Results are reported against the design goals. All figures are regenerable from the API calls in Appendix A against the deposited snapshot https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22303316.

6.1 DG1: composition and provenance

The corpus holds 279,485 occurrences assembled from 290,957 source records, of which 96.15 % derive from a single source, 3.61 % from two, 0.21 % from three and 0.02 % from four or more; the maximum for one occurrence is five, reached by 2 occurrences. That distribution bounds what the linkage can be said to achieve: only 10,757 occurrences (3.85 %) are attested by more than one authority, so every cross-source comparison in this paper rests on that subset rather than on the whole, and the remaining 268,728 single-sourced occurrences cannot be cross-checked within the corpus at all. Table 3 gives counts by decade, and Figure 1 the annual series. The corpus carries 131,882 non-empty narratives that resolve to a canonical occurrence, drawn from 122 of the 128 source codes and attached to 128,483 occurrences — 46.0 %. The two counts differ because several narratives can attach to one event, and quoting the narrative count against the occurrence total would overstate coverage by about a point. The narrative store holds 164,068 records in all; the residual 31,933 were fetched for source records that no occurrence in this snapshot claims — typically a native identifier the ingest saw but the occurrence loader did not — and a further 253 resolve but carry no text. They are counted here rather than dropped silently, and narrative_index.csv.gz in the snapshot lets any of these figures be recomputed under a different definition. The region column is empty for every row, so the geographic breakdown below is derived from country_iso, which is itself absent on 25.2 % of occurrences.

duplicates and no errors reads as settled, but in all forty the deciding signal

roughly 7.5 % by the rule of three, which over the full band admits
several hundred wrong merges.

Sample 2 was drawn deliberately from the strata sample 1 had missed and found
four genuine false merges in thirty. That cell — a mass-built airframe of a

Sample 3 is the one that matters, being the first drawn from the band that

One error in 59 confident verdicts is 1.7 %, and at that sample
size a single error constrains the rate only loosely: the exact binomial
(Clopper–Pearson) 95 % interval runs from 0.04 % to
9.1 %. Applied to the 3,441 pairs the band merges, the point
estimate is of order 58 false merges and the interval admits anywhere
from 1 to 313. That figure is reported here as history — it
describes the band as it stood when sample 3 was drawn, before the guards, and
it is superseded for the shipping band by sample 4 below. It is a point
estimate on 59 verdicts and never a bound: sixty adjudications cannot
exclude an error rate several times the one observed.

That figure describes the band sample 3 was drawn from, not the band that ships
today. Both problematic pairs are now demoted to the review queue by the guards
they produced: in the deposited merge table each carries status
pending at score 0.20, with evidence
model-number-conflict and collision respectively. Of the
sixty pairs of sample 3, 56 are still merged automatically and all
56 were adjudicated duplicates — but that is in-sample by
construction, because the guards were derived from the very two pairs they now
catch, and it cannot stand as a rate for the current band. Four pairs left
the automatic band, not two, and only three of them left it because of a
guard: besides the two the guards were derived from, a merge this sample had
adjudicated as a duplicate now sits at score 0.50 carrying
collision. The fourth, also adjudicated a duplicate, carries no guard
marker at all — its evidence string ends in high-toll-anchor, which
is a scoring bonus and not a guard, borne by 1,322 of the
3,441 rows the band applies — so at score 0.65 it falls below
the cut-off on the score alone. Put in the units the benefit is put in, the
guards withheld one correct merge to withdraw one wrong merge and one pair
that could not be decided. That is a cost in
recall, visible at all only because these sixty pairs happen to have been
adjudicated.

Sample 4 was drawn to answer that. It is a uniform random sample of 60
of the 3,441 pairs the band applies today, drawn with seed
20260901 so the selection can be repeated, and adjudicated out of
sample: none of the sixty is a pair any guard was derived from. All 60
were duplicates. Fifty-three were settled mechanically — the two sides carry
the same registration on the same date, in one case encoded in the absorbed
record’s own key rather than in its fields, and a registration is not reissued
within a day — and the remaining seven by reading the sources’ own records,
with the deciding fact recorded per pair. That split limits what the sample
establishes. Registration and date agreement is the signal the merge rule
already weighs most heavily, so for those fifty-three the adjudication confirms
the rule’s own input rather than re-establishing the event independently. Only
the seven decided from the sources’ records bring evidence the rule did not
use.

Zero errors in sixty does not mean the band is clean; it bounds how unclean it
can be. The exact (Clopper–Pearson) two-sided 95 % upper bound on
the rate is 5.96 %, which across the 3,441 applied pairs is
205 false merges; the one-sided 95 % bound, appropriate if
only the upper direction is of interest, is 4.87 % or 168
pairs. Neither is a ceiling on the number of false merges: both are confidence
statements about the rate, and a reader who wants the two-sided bound below
1 % would have to adjudicate 368 pairs at zero errors
(299 one-sided). That bound is
wider than the point estimate sample 3 gave, so these two samples together do
not demonstrate that the guards improved anything — sixty observations cannot
show that. What sample 4 establishes is narrower and was previously missing
altogether: the band as it ships has now been measured on a sample whose
selection is recorded, and it produced no error.

The figure bounds clerical
error only: the two global compilations dominating this cohort are not
independent of each other, so a duplicate that both inherited from one upstream
mistake would be adjudicated as a duplicate and counted as correct.

7 Discussion

7.1 What the multi-source share means

10,757 occurrences (3.85 %) carry records from more than one
source. That share is the corpus’s own cross-check surface — the only place
where two authorities can be observed describing one event — and it is the
quantity the merge work of Section 4.5 moves. Before the
adjudicated merges were applied the same corpus reported 7,558
multi-source occurrences (2.67 % of a then-larger total); the merges
raised the count by 42 %, not by finding new records but by
recognising that pairs already present described one event. Like the
recovery-pass figures of Section 4.6, this is a
run-time measurement of a state the deposit no longer holds: the snapshot
carries the corpus after those merges, so 7,558 cannot be recomputed
from it and is reported as measured rather than as checkable.

That figure answers the natural objection to spending this much care on
linkage in a corpus where 96 % of occurrences come from a single
source. The linkage does not change the single-source majority. What it
changes is the size of the minority on which every cross-source claim in this
paper and its companions rests, and it changed it by nearly half.

One internal quantity is reported in place of the external coverage estimate
that Section 5.2 explains cannot be computed: the
count of source records that reach an occurrence shared with another
authority, against those the ladder placed on a key of their own. That
measures the pipeline, not the world, and the distinction is the point of
Section 5.2.

7.2 What the corpus is and is not

The corpus is a harmonized, provenance-preserving aggregation of published official records. The design goals commit it to breadth, to a measured deduplication error, to a small common vocabulary and to reproducibility; they do not commit it to completeness, which no aggregation of published records can achieve, or to correctness beyond that of its sources. The validation results should be read in that light. The band that merges automatically was measured by a recorded random draw of 60 of the 3,441 pairs it applies, which found no error, bounding rather than disproving a rate: the exact two-sided 95 % upper bound is 6.0 %, a confidence statement about the rate and not a ceiling on the count. Recall is not measured at all, and that omission is the more serious of the two, because the ladder errs toward leaving records apart, so the duplicates it fails to join stay in the corpus as separate occurrences and inflate counts. Only the measured error can be placed on a scale — 205 pairs at that bound, 0.07 % of 279,485 occurrences, far below the between-source coverage differences visible in Figure 3. No comparable statement is possible for the unmeasured one, and nothing in this paper should be read as bounding it. The measured side can be tightened by users who need it: occurrence_merges.csv publishes every proposed pair with its status, its rule-derived score and the evidence string that produced it, so the 627 pairs still in the review queue can be adjudicated further and the applied band re-cut — resolving identifiers through event_sources.csv.gz, since 85 rows name a survivor the ladder has since re-keyed (Section 4.5). What is not published is a calibrated weight, because none is estimated.

7.3 Alternative explanations for the validation results

Several artefacts could produce the numbers in Section 6 without the pipeline working as intended. An error rate that flatters the band. Sample 1 measured 40 of 40 and meant less than it appeared, because its frame excluded the strata where errors live; the guard against this is not a wider sample but a stated frame, and Table 4 gives the frame of each. Adjudication agreeing with the corpus by construction. The two global compilations dominating the unkeyable cohort read the same official reports, so a duplicate both inherited from one upstream transcription error is adjudicated as a duplicate and counted correct; the reported rate therefore bounds clerical error and not source error, and no design available here separates them. Under-merging read as precision. The ladder errs toward leaving records apart, so a corpus that merged nothing would report no false merges at all — which is why the multi-source share of Section 7.1 is reported beside the error rate: it is the quantity that falls if the linkage becomes timid. Ingestion lag mistaken for under-reporting. Low apparent coverage for a state may mean an authority publishes in a form the adapters do not yet parse; the per-source coverage window in Table S1 separates the two.

7.4 Comparison with prior resources

Relative to ASN and B3A, the corpus is broader in source and carries their editorial judgement with them: it holds the national authorities’ records that neither compilation rewrites, and it also contains the events they record that no ingested authority describes, because both compilations are ingested as sources rather than filtered against an official list. It does not claim to be broader in aircraft class, since the ASN Wikibase it ingests already extends to light aircraft and rotorcraft. On this snapshot 58,425 occurrences (20.9 %) have no contributing source other than the four compilations — 21,217 carry only ASN, 31,558 only B3A, 55,770 nothing beyond those two — and these are what give the corpus its coverage outside investigated events. The deposit cannot separate compilation entries that cite an official report from those built on press coverage; a user who needs authority-level evidence should treat compilation-only occurrences as a stratum and filter on event_sources.csv.gz, as Section 5.2 already does for the coverage argument. Relative to the national databases, the corpus adds cross-reference and a common vocabulary at the cost of the field depth each authority provides natively; the source-specific fields are not carried over — the API’s facts{} object is a fixed summary of canonical values, not a per-source field store — so a study needing native depth must follow the URL in event_sources back to the source. Omrani et al. [10] found that models trained on NTSB, TSB and ATSB separately behave differently because of coding differences. This corpus does not resolve that: the allow-list of Section 4.9 collapses every unrecognized national class into accident and keeps no record that it did, so the coding differences are erased rather than reconciled, and occurrence_type must not be used as the outcome of a cross-authority comparison. What the corpus does add for that purpose is the source axis: the overlap matrix identifies the events several authorities described, and event_sources names which, so a study of coding disagreement can be run on the raw records behind those occurrences — retaining the pre-merge class is listed under future work in Section 6.3. The corpus follows the descriptor precedent set for aviation by the OpenSky datasets [11] in pairing a live API with frozen, DOI-referenced snapshots, and adopts the datasheet structure of Gebru et al. [19] in Appendix B. The author’s earlier open aggregate of the FAA wildlife-strike database [23] used the same versioned-release pattern for a single source; the present work extends it to many.

7.5 Intended uses and cautions

The corpus is suited to questions in which source attribution matters or in which breadth across jurisdictions outweighs field depth: cross-national comparison of occurrence mix, studies of disagreement between authorities, text mining over narratives with per-source licensing respected, and as a linkage target for other datasets keyed on registration and date. It is not suited, on its own, to rate estimation, because it carries no exposure denominators [8], or to trend estimation across the coverage window of any source without conditioning on that window, because Figure 1 shows that the series is dominated by when authorities began publishing. Users should stratify by source and occurrence class before pooling, should treat single-source occurrences from sources with no overlap as unvalidated, and should treat narratives under excerpt policy as pointers to the original rather than as text data.

Three aggregations should not be attempted on this snapshot at all. They are named here rather than left to be discovered downstream, and each follows from a property stated elsewhere in this paper. Do not sum fatalities_total over the corpus. The unit is one aircraft’s involvement and not the event (Section 3.2), so where an authority reports a collision’s combined toll on each airframe’s record the toll is counted once per occurrence; the sum is in addition conditional on the 52.5 % of occurrences that carry the field. The deposit permits neither the double count nor the missing half to be corrected, so a corpus-wide death toll is not a quantity this snapshot can produce. Do not cut a country time series across 2008. The current NTSB feed does not populate country_iso, so the “United States” column of Table 3 collapses at that handover for a reason that has nothing to do with aviation (Section 6.1). The break is a defect of this snapshot, and any per-state series spanning it measures the defect. Do not compute figures by aircraft type. type_icao resolves on 15.6 % of occurrences, and the residue is not missing at random: resolution runs from 25.7 % on the largest NTSB export down to 1.5 % on B3A and 0 on Wikidata, so a per-type count is a count of the sources whose model strings the lookup table happens to cover.

7.6 Limitations

7.6.1 One source is redistributed without a licence

Provenance is this corpus’s claim, so a gap in it is reported first, and the
gap is wider than a count of non-empty license fields suggests. The
field is free text, and across the 128 contributing codes it falls into
four classes. 15 codes (132,541 source records) name
recognisable terms — CC BY 4.0, CC0, the UK Open Government Licence,
Licence Ouverte/Etalab 2.0, United States public domain, the SKYbrary licence
agreement, and the Government of Canada non-commercial reproduction terms.
107 codes (127,436 records) carry nothing but the name of the
publishing organisation, among them ntsbpre (60,400) and
baaa (41,317); the corpus asserts a rights-holder for these,
not a grant. 5 codes (6,183 records) carry a descriptive note
about the material rather than terms. And one records an explicit absence.
The Aviation Safety Network publishes no usable terms: its footer copyright
link resolves to a 404, leaving a liability disclaimer and a copyright notice,
and the records this corpus ingests come from the user-contributed ASN Wikibase
rather than the curated ASN database. The registry entry therefore records the
copyright holder and the absence of a grant instead of asserting terms that
were never given. What this corpus asserts, then, is that it has recorded
whatever each source published about reuse; it does not assert that
127 of 128 sources have granted redistribution rights, and the
107 bare organisation names should be read as an open question per
source rather than as a permission.

The exposure is not small and is stated rather than buried: ASN supplies
24,797 source records (8.5 %) and is the primary source of
21,242 occurrences (7.6 %), so its field values are
redistributed in the snapshot. Facts about an accident — its date,
registration, location and toll — are not themselves protectable, and every
ASN-derived record carries a link back to the ASN page it came from; but a
substantial extraction from a compiled database engages the European
sui generis database right independently of copyright in the facts, and
this paper does not claim that question is settled. A permission request to the
Flight Safety Foundation is outstanding. Users who need to exclude the source
can: event_sources.csv.gz carries one row per contributing source, so
dropping source = ’aviation-safety’ and the occurrences left with no
other source is a two-line filter. Reproduced on this snapshot it removes the
24,797 ASN rows and 21,217 ASN-only occurrences, leaving
258,268. The filter does not make the remainder free of ASN, and it is
not offered as such: exactly 25 surviving occurrences still carry
aviation-safety as their primary source, all of them ASN and SKYbrary
together with the rank tie broken by first arrival, and a further 3,580
carry an ASN row beside a higher-ranked source, so some of their null fields may
have been filled from it. events.jsonl.gz holds no per-field
provenance, so that filling cannot be undone from the deposit.

7.6.2 Fields that are mostly empty

The schema is wider than the data. Any cut along a sparse field silently
changes its own denominator, so the completeness of every variable used in this
paper is given here rather than in a supplement, measured on the deposited
snapshot: event_date and
report_url 100 %,
registration 78.0 %, country_iso 74.8 %,
phase 70.1 %, category 58.9 %,
fatalities_total 52.5 %, operator 41.5 %,
occupants 28.2 %, lat 16.9 %,
type_icao 15.6 %, flight_nature 14.8 % and
manufacturer 0.4 %. Three fields named elsewhere in this paper
are returned by the API but are not columns of events.jsonl, so the
snapshot supports no figure for them and none is quoted:
cause_summary, airline_icao and region. The last is
null on every row the API returns and is discussed immediately below.

Three of these deserve naming rather than tabulating. region is empty
for every occurrence, so no regional analysis is possible from this corpus
without inferring region from country_iso, which is itself missing on
a quarter of records and missing most on the recent decades — there because
the current NTSB feed’s normalizer leaves country_iso null, a defect
of this snapshot rather than of the source. type_icao
at 15.6 % means a per-type rate computed from this corpus describes the
sixth of it that carries a designator, not the fleet. And
fatalities_total at 52.5 % means that a sum of deaths over the
corpus is a sum over the half of records that report them; the figure quoted
anywhere in this paper is that conditional sum and not an estimate of deaths.

7.6.3 Consistency findings, not future checks

The integrity checks were run rather than planned, and three of them return
non-zero. 4,945 occurrences sit at latitude and longitude both exactly zero, and 6,312 lie within the 0.05^{\circ} radius the key ladder treats as unknown,
which is not a position in the Gulf of Guinea but a source’s way of writing
“unknown”; the key ladder already refuses to link on such a position, but a
map built from this corpus without filtering will draw them. 176
occurrences are fatal but not typed accident, of which 169 are
combat_loss and 7 incident. 90 registrations
appear in more than five distinct occurrences, which is plausible for a
long-lived training airframe and is also what a normalisation error looks like;
they are flagged, not resolved. Future-dated events: 0.

7.6.4 One state, four source codes

Four NTSB source codes supply 60.8 % of source records. The corpus is
therefore a United States dataset with substantial international coverage
rather than a global one with an American component, and every unqualified
aggregate in it inherits that shape. This is not correctable by weighting: the
absent records are absent because the authorities that would have filed them
publish nothing machine-readable, and a weight cannot recover an event nobody
wrote down.

The most important limitation is coverage bias inherited from the sources: events that no source published in any form are absent, and International Air Transport Association and International
Coordinating Council of Aerospace Industries Associations and International
Federation of Air Line Pilots’ Associations [3] show that non-publication of final reports is concentrated in particular states. Their figure bounds report availability rather than corpus coverage — an event without a final report may still enter through a preliminary report, a reporting system or a compilation — but the concentration by state is the relevant warning either way. The corpus cannot estimate this from within at all; Section 5.2 explains why the natural external check is unavailable. Second, the linkage relies on registration and date; events where the registration is absent from all sources, or where an authority reports only a month, depend on the weaker blocking keys and have lower recall, which is concentrated in older records and in sources with sparse structured fields. Third, the mapping from native classes to three harmonized levels loses information and, for sources with a single undifferentiated class, imposes a severity-based rule that Annex 13 does not sanction; the native class is not retained in the published objects — neither the API’s facts{} nor events.jsonl.gz carries it — so the canonical class must be read as the output of that default rule and the native class recovered from the source. Fourth, the samples were adjudicated with a large language model under the author’s direction (see Assisted work), working from the sources’ own published records rather than independently of the project; adjudication by the party that built the linkage is common in this literature [17] but it is not independent, and the reported rates should be read with that in mind. Fifth, narratives are multilingual and are not translated, and the corpus makes no attempt to remove names of individuals that authorities themselves publish. Finally, the corpus is a moving target: sources are added and re-ingested, thresholds may be re-tuned, and the figures in this paper describe the snapshot deposited as version 2026-09-04 (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.22303316) and no other.

7.7 Future work

Three extensions follow naturally. Exposure denominators from fleet and schedule data would convert counts into rates and are the subject of a companion paper in preparation. Field-level disagreement between authorities, of which the class disagreement in Section 6.3 is one instance, is analyzed in a second companion paper on cross-source disagreement, in preparation. Structured extraction from narratives [12] could recover the source-specific detail the harmonized schema drops — phase of flight in particular, carried today only as the source’s own unmapped string — into the ADREP vocabulary; a pipeline for that is evaluated separately in a companion paper on narrative text mining, in preparation. On the linkage side, replacing the point-estimate thresholds by a posterior over partitions [16] would allow downstream counts to carry linkage uncertainty. Two smaller changes would answer questions this paper has to leave open. Persisting each contributing record’s pre-merge occurrence class would make the cross-source disagreement rate of Section 6.3 measurable instead of unrecoverable; it is a schema change, not an analysis. And recall, which is not measured here at all, admits a cheap internal lower bound: mask the registrations on a sample of the 10,757 occurrences already linked by registration and count how many the scoring pass recovers without them. That would not measure the duplicates no source keyed, but it would replace an unmeasured quantity with a measured floor.

8 Conclusion

The FlightFinder Occurrence Corpus assembles 279,485 aviation occurrences and 131,882 attached narratives from the 128 source codes that contribute to it — national investigation authorities and occurrence-reporting systems, four global compilations, and one public-records channel — into one schema, with the identity and URL of one record per contributing source preserved on each occurrence — provenance is keyed on (occurrence, source), so where one source supplies two records of the same occurrence only one survives in provenance — and the attribution and licence terms carried on the source code it belongs to. The construction pipeline is documented stage by stage: per-source ingestion into a staging table keyed on source and native identifier and overwritten on re-sync, normalization of dates, registrations, aircraft types and locations, a deterministic key ladder in which each record reduces to exactly one identifier so that a missing field cannot create a link, an explicit merge table for the cohort the ladder cannot reach, an authority-priority rule for canonical values, and a closed allow-list with a stated fall-through default for occurrence type and severity — which is why accident at 99.17 % is a property of that default and not of aviation. On the band that merges automatically, a recorded random draw of 60 found no error, bounding its false-merge rate at 6.0 % with 95 % confidence; coverage relative to the ASN and B3A compilations is not reported, because both are ingested by the corpus and the comparison would measure a subset of it against itself; see Section 5.2 for why no capture–recapture estimate of the events missed by every list is offered. The corpus carries no exposure data and inherits the under-reporting of its sources, and it is offered not as a complete census of aviation occurrences but as a documented, provenance-preserving base whose reported statistics can be recomputed from the deposited snapshot on which the coverage and disagreement of the world’s investigation systems can themselves be studied. A frozen snapshot, the adjudicated linkage sample and the source registry accompany the live API so that every table in this paper can be regenerated and every future release compared with it.

Data and code availability

The corpus is served by the himaxym.com data API, base URL https://himaxym.com/api/v1/data, documented by the OpenAPI specification at https://himaxym.com/api/v1/data/openapi.json. The endpoints used in this paper are GET /sources (registry), GET /events (paginated occurrences with filters), GET /events/{id} (occurrence with all contributing sources and narratives), GET /narratives/{source}/{id} and GET /aircraft/{family}/safety. The GET /sources and GET /events endpoints answer without credentials at a low rate; sustained use requires a free API key (1,000 requests per day, 60 per minute) or a Pro key (20,000 per day); full narrative text under full policy is available on the Pro tier only. Structured fields are redistributed under the licence stated per source code in the license field of the registry, returned inline on each element of sources[] by GET /events/{id}; narrative text is redistributed only under the source’s policy, and users must reproduce the attribution string. The frozen snapshot described in this paper, comprising the occurrence and source tables, the registry, the narrative index and the adjudicated linkage sample with its verdicts, is deposited at Zenodo under concept DOI 10.5281/zenodo.22228451, which always resolves to the most recent version; the exact snapshot from which every number reported here is computed is version DOI 10.5281/zenodo.22303316. The deposit also carries the two scripts that regenerate the manuscript’s descriptives and figures from the deposited files, so every reported number can be recomputed without access to the live database. The ingestion, normalization and linkage modules are not in the deposit: they are part of a larger application that is not public, and are specified in the construction and linkage sections above rather than released; the deposited merge table records every linkage decision they produced. For the corpus to be rebuildable from source records, a future release would need to ship either the scoring module itself as a standalone executable with its dictionaries — the placeholder word list, the type–token fold table, the collision markers and the boilerplate patterns — or a complete numeric specification of it, every rule bonus, the high-toll anchor’s definition and trigger toll, the guard mechanics and the band cut-off, with worked test pairs whose scores and statuses can be checked against occurrence_merges.csv. The deposited files are released under CC BY 4.0 except where the source’s own terms forbid it, and three sources are carved out by name: the Transportation Safety Board of Canada (1,317 records, Crown copyright reproduced under the Government of Canada non-commercial reproduction terms), SKYbrary (464 records, under a bespoke EUROCONTROL agreement) and the Aviation Safety Network (24,797 records, which grants nothing — see Section 7.6.1). Rows from those three are redistributed under their own terms and are not relicensed here. All structured fields remain subject to the per-source attribution recorded in Table S1.

Appendix A Reproducibility protocol

The following steps regenerate every table and figure from the API and the snapshot. Three levels of reproduction must be kept apart, and only the first is supported here. (i) Re-deriving this paper’s statistics from the snapshot is supported in full: the deposit and describe.mjs deliver every number reported above. (ii) Rebuilding the corpus from source records is not supported: the ingestion, normalization and scoring modules are closed (Section Data and code availability), and the numeric specification of the scoring pass is not published. (iii) Reversing a merge is possible only as far as naming it: event_sources.csv.gz carries (occurrence_id, source, native_id, url) and no field values, so a reader can identify the absorbed source record and retrieve it from its source, but cannot recover the values it held in the corpus. Environment: Node.js 24.x for the pipeline itself, which has no statistical dependencies — the linkage is a key ladder and a rule-based scoring pass, so there is no model to fit, no library to pin and no seed to fix. No Python and no version pinning are required: describe.mjs and make_figures.mjs in the deposit use only the Node standard library, and both resolve their inputs next to themselves, so they run unchanged from the flat Zenodo download (node describe.mjs) or from a checkout (node deposit/describe.mjs), and the figures are emitted as SVG and converted with rsvg-convert

1. 

Registry. curl -H "Authorization: Bearer $KEY" https://himaxym.com/api/v1/data/sources returns the registry (code, name, license, homepage, narratives, policy). Save as sources.json. Note that the registry and the corpus are not the same list: the registry is the set of codes permitted to enter, and at the time of this snapshot two of them (bdca, dinacpy) had contributed no records yet. Table S1 and the source axis of Figure 3 are the 128 codes that actually contribute, which is event_sources.csv.gz grouped by source — take that as the definition, not the registry’s row count.

2. 

Occurrences. Page through GET /events?from=YYYY-MM-DD&to=2026-MM-DD&limit=100&cursor=... until next_cursor is null, writing each data[] element to events.jsonl. The snapshot contains the identical table and needs no paging. Optional filters country, type, family, operator, fatal=1 are not used for the full pull.

3. 

Contributing sources. For each occurrence, GET /events/{id} returns sources[] and narratives[], each source element carrying source, attribution, license and url. To avoid 279,485 calls the snapshot ships a reduced projection of the same relation, event_sources.csv.gz, with columns (occurrence_id,source,native_id,url) only. Attribution and licence are not repeated per row: they are properties of the source code, and the snapshot carries them once per code in table_S1.csv, which is joined on source. A reader who needs licence terms at record level must perform that join, or call the API, where the fields are returned inline.

4. 

Narratives. The snapshot carries no narrative text — licences differ per source and several permit excerpting only — but it ships narrative_index.csv.gz, one row per stored narrative with columns (occurrence_id,source,native_id,slug,narrative_chars,probable_cause_chars,indexable). Every narrative count in this paper is a filter over that file: 164,068 rows in all, 132,135 whose occurrence_id is non-null (the narrative resolves to a canonical occurrence), and 131,882 of those with non-empty text — the figure quoted in Section 6.1, covering 128,483 occurrences from 122 source codes. Text itself is retrieved per record through GET /narratives/{source}/{id} under that source’s policy.

5. 

Deduplication rules (for rebuilding from source records). Normalize registration as upper(regexp_replace(reg,’[^A-Z0-9]’,’’)) after stripping a trailing parenthesized suffix, and reject the placeholder word list before using the result. Parse dates to a local calendar date, retaining internally whether a day, a month or only a year was resolved; that flag is not a field of the published schema. Then apply the ladder of Section 4.4 in order, taking the first rung that applies — event signature for the three tier-aware sources, registration and date, position rounded to one decimal and date, source and native identifier — and group records by the resulting key. There is no model to fit, no threshold to sweep and no seed to fix. For the cohort that reaches the last rung, the scoring rules of Section 4.6 are stated as rules but not as numbers, so this step does not reconstruct the bands: the per-rule bonuses, the definition and trigger toll of the high-toll anchor, the guard demotion mechanics and the band cut-off are not published, and occurrence_merges.csv in the snapshot is therefore the authority for the status and evidence string of every proposed pair rather than a check on a re-derivation.

6. 

Canonical values. Take the record from the highest-ranked source (Section 4.8: rank 3 uniform authorities, rank 2 MAK and BAAA, rank 1 Wikidata, ASN and SKYbrary), then fill its null fields from the records it beat. The rank is a property of the source code, not of the row, so it is not a column of event_sources.csv: join on source to obtain it. Break ties by first arrival, except among the three tier-aware sources of Section 4.4, where a final report outranks an interim one and an interim a preliminary; publication date plays no part.

7. 

Derived variables. severity = fatal if fatalities_total >0 , else per the rule in Section 4.9; region is not derived from anything — it is null on every row of the published corpus and no step in this protocol produces it.

8. 

Table 1 and 3. For Table 1, group event_sources.csv by source and count rows, taking the year window from the joined occurrences. For Table 3, group events.jsonl by decade of event_date and by country_iso collapsed to three columns — US, any other code, and null — keeping null as its own column rather than distributing it. Neither table is grouped by region; see Section 7.6.2.

9. 

Linkage evaluation. Load occurrence_merges.csv (snapshot), 4,068 rows with columns (absorbed_id,survivor_id,status,method,score,evidence,decided_by,decided_at): 3,441 rows are auto and 627 pending. The decided_by and decided_at columns are empty on every row — the pipeline never populated them, so the merge table carries no adjudication trail and the audit trail is adjudicated_pairs.csv alone; all adjudication was LLM-assisted under the author’s supervision, as declared under Assisted work. There is no weight file and no threshold to sweep, and the band assignment cannot be reproduced from this paper (step 7): take the status column of occurrence_merges.csv as given and reproduce the reported error rates by comparing the five adjudicated samples against those bands as in Table 4. The file is consistent with a cut-off of 0.80 on score combined with the fan guard — the lowest auto score is 0.80 and all 270 pending rows at or above it carry fan-demoted — but that is an inference from the deposit, not a published rule. Sample 4 is additionally re-drawable: take the 3,441 rows with status auto in the order they appear in the file and draw 60 with Python’s random.sample after random.seed(20260901); the draw is generator-specific, so another language’s RNG seeded with the same number selects a different set. Those samples are deposited as adjudicated_pairs.csv, 200 rows with columns (sample,pair_id,absorbed_id,survivor_id,event_date,fatalities,verdict,basis); the error rates in Table 4 are counts over that file and need no estimator. There is no cluster file and no stratified interval to compute — see Section 5.1 for why neither exists. Figure 2 is not built from these files: it counts sources per occurrence in event_sources.csv.gz and is described there because it stands in place of the match-weight histogram this design cannot produce.

10. 

Coverage against ASN and B3A. Not reproduced — see Section 5.2 for why the comparison is not reported: both compilations are ingested by the corpus, so the check would measure a subset of the corpus against itself.

11. 

Overlap matrix. From event_sources.csv, self-join on occurrence_id to count occurrences per source pair; order by contribution and show raw counts, the diagonal being each source’s total; Figure 3.

12. 

Internal checks. Run node describe.mjs (snapshot) to regenerate descriptives.json, which carries the completeness and consistency statistics of Section 5.4. It reads events.jsonl.gz and event_sources.csv.gz and uses the Node standard library only; make_figures.mjs regenerates the three figures from the same two files.

Appendix B Datasheet summary

Following Gebru et al. [19], this appendix is the dataset’s datasheet; the deposit carries no separate datasheet file. Motivation: to provide a provenance-preserving, cross-jurisdictional occurrence dataset for safety research; created by the author; no external funding. Composition: 279,485 occurrence records and 131,882 attached narrative records (164,068 in the narrative store overall); instances are investigated or reported aviation occurrences; the dataset is a sample of the population of occurrences, biased as described in Section 3.4; narratives may contain names of individuals as published by the source authorities; no labels beyond the harmonized fields. Collection: retrieved from public official sources by scripted adapters, most recently 2026-08; no individuals were contacted. Preprocessing: as in Section 4; source records are held in a staging table at their state as of the last re-sync, and earlier states are not retained. Uses: safety statistics, source-comparison studies, text mining; not suitable for rate estimation without external denominators or for identifying individuals. Distribution: API and Zenodo snapshot; per-source licences. Maintenance: re-ingested on per-source schedules; versioned releases with changelogs; errata accepted at the corresponding author.
